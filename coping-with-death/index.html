You’re not alone here.

Sometimes, while carrying the heavy things, we don’t see how far we’ve come.

Start here

You don’t need perfect words. Kids often do best with: clear truth, steady presence, and permission to feel. You can return to this page as often as you need.

Right now (30 seconds)

Put a hand on chest or belly. Breathe in for 4, out for 6 (twice). Name 3 things you see. Tell yourself: “This is hard, and I’m here.”

Age guide (pick the lane that fits)

Kids grieve in bursts. This isn’t a “right way” — it’s just a shortcut to language that usually lands.

Ages 3–6 (simple + concrete)
  • What they understand: death can feel confusing; they may think it’s temporary or caused by something they did.
  • What helps: short sentences, repetition, routines, and lots of reassurance.
  • Try saying: “They died. Their body stopped working. They can’t come back.”
  • Watch for: clinginess, toilet/sleep changes, “play grief” (repeating scenes). Normal.
  • One tiny support: a comfort object + a predictable bedtime check-in (“I’ll come back in 5 minutes.”)
Ages 7–12 (questions + fairness)
  • What they understand: death is permanent — but they may ask why a lot and look for reasons.
  • What helps: honest answers, choices (funeral, rituals), and “two-track” permission (sad + still have fun).
  • Try saying: “We can talk now, later, or in tiny pieces. All versions are okay.”
  • Watch for: anger, guilt, “I don’t care” masking, stomach aches, school focus issues.
  • One tiny support: a “question parking lot” list — write questions down so they don’t have to hold them alone.
Teens (privacy + big meaning)
  • What they understand: the facts — but the meaning can hit hard (identity, purpose, “what’s the point?”).
  • What helps: respectful space, choice, and low-pressure connection (drive, walk, gaming, music).
  • Try saying: “You don’t have to talk. I’ll check in. I’m here even if you’re quiet.”
  • Watch for: risk-taking, shutting down, irritability, sleep changes, substance use, isolation.
  • One tiny support: agree on a signal — emoji/text that means “I’m not okay” without needing a long talk.

Type of death (the words change depending on what happened)

You can keep it truthful without giving details that feel too big. If a child asks for details you’re not ready for, you can say: “That’s a real question. I’m going to answer it in a safe way.”

Sudden / unexpected death
  • Core line: “It happened suddenly. We didn’t get to prepare. That can feel shocking.”
  • If they ask ‘why?’: “Sometimes bodies stop working very fast. It’s not because of anything you did.”
  • Support move: name the normal reactions: “Your brain may keep replaying it. That’s a shock response.”
Expected / medical death (illness)
  • Core line: “Their body was very sick, and the doctors couldn’t fix it.”
  • What helps: explain the care: “They were cared for and not alone.”
  • Support move: invite mixed feelings: relief + sadness can both be true.
Suicide
Gentle note
  • Core line: “They died because of an illness in their brain that affected how they felt and thought.”
  • Important: avoid “they chose to leave us” language. Keep it health-based and compassionate.
  • Safety line: “When people feel trapped, they need help. If you ever feel like that, tell me — we get help.”
  • If asked ‘was it my fault?’: “No. This was not caused by you. Adults are responsible for getting help.”
Overdose / substance-related death
  • Core line: “They died because their body was affected by drugs/alcohol in a way that stopped it from working.”
  • Optional frame: “Addiction can be an illness that makes it hard to stop, even when people want to.”
  • If asked for details: “I’m going to keep it simple and safe. The important part is: they died, and we’re here.”
Violence / homicide
  • Core line: “Someone hurt them, and they died. It’s not okay, and it’s not your fault.”
  • Safety focus: “Right now, the adults are working on safety. Your job is to be a kid and be cared for.”
  • Limit details: give only what they need for safety, not graphic information.
When families have different beliefs (heaven, spirit, afterlife)
  • Two-track approach: “Their body died — and different people believe different things about what happens to the spirit.”
  • Invite the child: “What do you think? What feels comforting to you?”
  • Permission: it’s okay to say “I don’t know.” That’s honest and safe.

What to do next (tiny plans when your brain is overloaded)

You’re allowed to go into “minimum viable caregiving.” Basics count.

First 24 hours
  • Safety: If anyone feels unsafe, use 988 or 911 (Canada).
  • Basics: water + food + sleep attempt (even just “rest in bed”).
  • One trusted person: text/call someone who can anchor you (family, friend, elder, neighbour).
  • Keep language simple: “They died. Their body stopped working.”
  • Routines: keep one normal thing (breakfast, bedtime story, walk).
  • School/work: notify the school (you can use the School Kit below).
First week
  • Predictable check-in: “Each day we’ll do one small check-in. You can say ‘pass.’”
  • Choices: for rituals/funeral/memorial: attend / not attend / leave early / support person.
  • Firsts: name what’s coming (first bedtime, first school day, first weekend). “Waves may show up.”
  • Body stuff: appetite and sleep changes are common. Aim for “good enough.”
  • Connection: low-pressure time together (walk, movie, game). Not a deep talk.
  • Ask for help: meals, rides, childcare, dog walk — people want something concrete to do.
When grief spikes (bedtime, holidays, birthdays, school drop-off)
  • Name it: “This feels like a wave.”
  • Do a 60-second body reset: breathe out longer than in (twice) + feet on floor.
  • Offer 2 choices: “Do you want a hug or space? Do you want to talk or do something beside each other?”
  • Anchor line: “This is hard, and we’re safe right now.”
  • After: when it eases, do basics (water/snack) and return to routine.

School kit (copy/paste + printable)

This is meant to reduce the back-and-forth. You can send one message and be done for now.

Low effort, high impact
Email to school (draft)

You can edit the brackets. Keep it short. You don’t owe a lot of details.

Copy/paste

Subject: Support request for [Child’s Name] Hi [Teacher/School Team], I’m reaching out to let you know that [Child’s Name] is grieving a death in our family/community. We’re doing our best to keep routine, but there may be days where emotions spike, focus is harder, or they need a break. What would help at school: • A safe person they can check in with (or a quiet space if needed) • Permission for a short break pass (5 minutes) without making it a big deal • Gentle flexibility with deadlines/participation for the next little while • A quick heads-up if you notice major changes (withdrawal, panic, frequent tears, anger, or school refusal) If classmates ask questions, we’d prefer simple, respectful language and privacy. Thank you for supporting them — it really matters. [Your Name] [Phone/Email]

What teachers should know (quick list)
  • Grief comes in bursts. A child can be okay in math and fall apart at recess.
  • Common impacts: focus issues, irritability, fatigue, stomach aches, withdrawal, “acting younger,” or wanting control.
  • Support that works: predictable check-ins, private break options, and not forcing disclosure.
  • Helpful language: “I’m here. Do you want a break or to stay?”

Caregiver scripts (copy/paste ready)

Simple lines you can use when you don’t know what to say. Use your own voice, these are just scaffolding.

Clear, gentle truth
  • They died. That means their body stopped working and they can’t come back.
  • You didn’t cause this. Nothing you thought or said made it happen.
  • It’s okay to ask questions anytime. If I don’t know, we’ll find out together.
“Why did they die?” (when you don’t have all the answers)
  • That’s a real question. Sometimes bodies stop working because of sickness, accidents, or things we can’t control.
  • If I don’t know the full answer yet, I’ll tell you what I know — and I’ll tell you when I learn more.
  • What I do know is: you are safe right now, and you are not alone.
“Where are they now?” (different beliefs)
  • Their body died. Different people believe different things about what happens after.
  • In our family, we believe _______. Some people believe _______. It’s okay if you’re not sure.
  • Sometimes “where they are” feels like memories, love, stories, and the ways they shaped us.
When kids worry you’ll die too
  • I plan to be here for a long time. Right now, I’m okay.
  • If I ever get sick, we’ll talk about it and make a plan. You won’t be alone.
  • When your brain worries, come tell me. We can calm it together.
When grief comes in waves
  • This might feel big right now, and later it might feel quieter. Both are normal.
  • Waves can show up at bedtime, school drop-off, holidays, birthdays, and ‘firsts.’
  • The wave doesn’t mean you’re going backward, it means love is still here.
When kids don’t want to talk
  • You don’t have to talk. We can draw, walk, game, or sit together.
  • If words are hard, you can show me with a number: 0–10, how big is it today?
  • I’ll check in sometimes. You can tell me ‘not now’ and I’ll still stay close.
When you’re crying (and you don’t want to scare them)
  • My feelings are big because I loved them. Tears are one way my body lets feelings out.
  • You didn’t cause this, and you don’t need to fix it. I’m still here with you.
  • We can take a breath together. Want a hug, space, or a snack break?

Gentle prompts you can use with kids

Prompts that invite feelings and memories without forcing a big conversation.

Check-in

If your feelings had a weather forecast today, what would it be?

Body clues

Where do you feel the big feeling in your body; chest, throat, tummy, hands?

Memory & meaning

What’s one small thing you want to remember about them?

Permission

All feelings are allowed here, even the confusing ones.

Connection

Do you want to talk, draw, move, or just sit together?

One step

What’s one tiny thing that could help right now; water, snack, hug, walk, or quiet?

Grounding (5–4–3–2–1)

A quick way to help the brain return to the present when feelings spike.

5 things you can see

Look around slowly. Name them out loud if you can.

4 things you can touch

Feet on floor, hands on fabric, chair under you.

3 things you can hear

Near sounds, far sounds, tiny sounds.

2 things you can smell

If you can’t smell, name 2 smells you like.

1 thing you can taste

Water, gum, mint… or just notice your mouth.

Finish with one line

“This is hard, and I’m here.”

Grief basics + questions kids ask

Is it normal that they seem “fine” sometimes?
Yes. Kids often move in and out of grief. They can cry, then play, then cry again. That isn’t forgetting — it’s their nervous system taking breaks.
What if they keep asking the same questions?
Repetition is common. It helps them practice understanding. You can answer briefly, the same way each time, and offer connection: “Do you want a hug or to draw?”
Should we bring them to a funeral?
Often yes, with preparation and choice. Explain what they will see, offer a support person, and give permission to leave. If they don’t attend, consider a different ritual (letter, candle, memory box).
How do I explain death in a way kids understand?
Use clear words and avoid confusing metaphors. “Died” means the body stopped working and can’t start again. Then stay close and invite questions.
When should I get extra help?
Consider extra support if you notice persistent sleep changes, frequent panic, extreme withdrawal, ongoing school refusal, or if anyone feels unsafe. Trust your gut — support is allowed.

About this page

This page is meant to be a support + education tool — not a replacement for therapy, medical care, or crisis services. If you’re in a high-intensity moment, start with Grounding, then use one script or one checklist.

How to use this page (the “one thing” rule)
  • Pick one section: Age guide, Type of death, or Checklists.
  • Choose one script line that feels natural in your mouth.
  • Come back later. You don’t have to do grief “all at once.”
Privacy + details
You don’t owe people the full story. With kids, you can be truthful and still protect them from graphic detail. A safe boundary is: “I’m going to answer in a safe way.”
