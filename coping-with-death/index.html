Caregiver scripts

These are “ready-to-say” lines. You can copy them and use your own words. Kids benefit from clear language (no confusing euphemisms) and steady reassurance.

Copy + paste friendly

When you don’t know what to say

“I don’t have perfect words, but I’m here with you. We can talk about it a little at a time.”

Clear death language (gentle + honest)

“They died. That means their body stopped working, and they can’t come back. We can still love them and remember them.”

When a child feels responsible

“This is not your fault. Nothing you did, said, or thought made this happen. Grown-up things caused this.”

When feelings come in waves

“Big feelings can come back like waves. A wave doesn’t mean you’re failing. It means you miss someone who mattered.”

Bedtime check-in

“Nights can feel bigger. We can do one small thing: water, cuddle, a story, or a quiet song. I’ll stay close.”

“I’m here” (micro-script)

“I’m here. You’re safe with me. We’ll get through this moment together.”

Gentle prompts you can use with kids

Choose one. You don’t need to do them all. Some kids talk with words, some talk with their bodies, play, art, or silence.

Check-in

“If your feelings had a weather forecast today, what would it be?”

Body clues

“Where do you feel the big feeling; chest, throat, tummy, hands?”

Memory & meaning

“What’s one small thing you want to remember about them?”

Permission

“All feelings are allowed here, even the confusing ones.”

Choice

“Do you want to talk, draw, move, or just sit together?”

One small step

“What tiny thing could help right now; water, snack, hug, walk, or quiet?”

Prompts for teens (less “kid-ish”) tap
  • “Do you want solutions, distraction, or someone to just sit with you?”
  • “On a scale of 1–10, how heavy is today?”
  • “What’s the hardest time of day for you right now?”
  • “What would feel supportive: space, food, a drive, a show, a walk, or talking?”
  • “You don’t have to perform being okay. I can handle the truth.”

Grief basics (simple + true)

Grief doesn’t follow a straight line. It changes shape. It can return during milestones, seasons, school changes, birthdays, holidays, and “firsts.” That doesn’t mean you’re back at the start.

What kids often misunderstand about death tap
  • Permanence: younger kids may think death is reversible.
  • Cause: kids can believe thoughts/anger/wishes caused it (magical thinking).
  • Blame: guilt shows up easily (“If I had…”).
  • Safety: they may worry others will die soon too.
  • Body questions: “What happens to the body?” is normal curiosity, not disrespect.
What helps most (even when you feel helpless) tap
  • Consistency: regular routines, predictable care, same bedtime steps.
  • Clear language: “died” vs. “went to sleep” (sleep language can create fear).
  • Permission: “Any feeling is allowed. You’re not in trouble.”
  • Memory: stories, photos, rituals, a memory object, a small tradition.
  • Connection: more presence, fewer lectures.

Questions kids ask (and gentle ways to answer)

You can keep answers short. Follow their lead. If they ask again later, that’s normal.

“Where are they now?” tap

Match your family’s beliefs. If you’re unsure, try: “Different people believe different things. What do you think?” You can always add: “What I know for sure is we can still love them and remember them.”

“Did I cause it?” tap

Be direct: “No. This is not your fault.” Repeat as many times as needed. Kids often need to hear this more than once.

“Are you going to die too?” tap

Offer safety without false promises: “Most people live a long time. My plan is to be here with you. If anything changes, you will be cared for.”

“Why are we still sad?” tap

Normalize waves: “Sadness can come back because love doesn’t disappear. We can have okay moments and sad moments.”

“Can we talk about something else?” tap

Yes. Avoid forcing processing: “Absolutely. We can take a break. If you want to come back later, I’m here.”

Extra support

Canada

If you or your child/youth feels unsafe, overwhelmed, or at risk of harm, reach out for immediate support. In Canada you can call or text 988 for suicide crisis support, or call 911 in an emergency. For non-urgent support, consider a trusted person, your family doctor, a school counsellor, or local community grief supports.

Grounding

A quick reset for wave moments.

1) Feel your feet on the floor. 2) Name 3 things you can see. 3) Put a hand on your chest or belly. 4) Inhale 4… exhale 6 (twice). 5) Say: “This is hard, and I’m here.”
